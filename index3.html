My research studies how environmental buyback and retirement programs can be designed to change the behavior of producers and resource owners, using structural and spatial methods alongside laboratory experiments. I work across conservation contracts, land retirement, and common-pool resource buybacks, focusing on how spatial spillovers shape participation and cost-effectiveness.

Job Market Paper

How Spillovers Erode Participation in Conservation Buybacks: Evidence from the Texas Inshore Shrimp Fishery

Buyback programs meant to reduce overcapitalization can undercut their own progress: as capacity is removed, the agents who remain gain a less crowded resource and grow less willing to exit. Using the Texas inshore shrimp license buyback over 26 years, I estimate a two-stage structural model and find that for every thirteen active bidders, roughly one holds out (about 7.6%) as stock recovery makes staying more valuable. Because about two-thirds of retired licenses were already inactive, targeting matters: at the same budget, restricting purchases to active licenses would have retired 123 more trawl-days per license, and targeting San Antonio bay alone would have retired 306 more.

Buyback programs are widely used to reduce overcapitalization and generate social benefits in settings such as land retirement, groundwater conservation, and fisheries. Yet evaluations often stop at program boundaries, even though removals can generate spillovers: displaced activity may move elsewhere, while those who remain benefit from a replenished resource. I study how these gains feed back into buyback program participation. As capacity is removed, the continuation value of remaining participants rises, creating an incentive to hold out rather than exit. Successful removals can therefore weaken the program participation required for further success.

I quantify this feedback-loop using the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department's 26-year inshore shrimp license buyback as a testbed. I estimate a two-stage structural model of vessels' spatial fishing choices and license-retirement decisions, then use it to simulate continuation values and reconstruct harvest and auction activity when vessel-level data are unavailable. I find that for every thirteen active license-holders who bid, roughly one who would otherwise have participated holds out as stock recovery makes staying more valuable. Program effectiveness is further constrained by two margins: approximately two-thirds of retired licenses were already inactive, and surviving vessels replace more of the retired effort in denser bay systems. At the same budget, restricting purchases to active licenses would have retired 123 more trawl-days per license, while targeting San Antonio bay alone would have retired 306 more. Together, the results show that conservation buybacks can undermine participation and additionality through the benefits they create for those who remain.

Strategic Delay, Signaling, and Risk Preferences in Agglomeration Bonus Mechanisms

Environmental and Resource Economics, revise & resubmit

Many environmental and resource policies rely on group-based incentives that require individuals to make binding participation decisions with payoffs that depend on the actions of others. A prominent example is agglomeration bonus schemes, which reward participants more as neighboring agents also adopt. In such settings, uncertainty about future coordination creates incentives to delay commitment, particularly when participation is irreversible and outcomes are risky. This paper studies how irreversibility, peer information, and risk preferences jointly shape the timing of adoption in coordination-based incentive schemes.

I test how these mechanisms interact in a laboratory experiment in which participants decide whether to make an irreversible commitment to a group-dependent payoff. The design exogenously varies the visibility of peers' past choices and independently elicits risk preferences. Time-to-adoption estimates show that observed peer adoption substantially accelerates uptake: each additional accumulated observed adoption shortens time to adoption by 0.5–1.7 percent. Risk preferences significantly delay adoption, but only under a higher-stakes payoff structure. A simulation combining these estimates suggests that expanding peer visibility yields program benefits equivalent to roughly 0.2 additional adopters per participant at empirically documented risk aversion levels. Together, the results highlight the importance of information design for agglomeration bonus schemes, and suggest that peer visibility yields the greatest returns in populations facing higher-stakes, irreversible participation decisions.
Work in Progress

Endogenous Entry and Bid Behavior in Common-Pool Resource Buyback Auctions

In progress

Building on the structural framework of my job market paper, this project models bidding and endogenous entry directly, drawing on the auction-theoretic literature on entry into procurement and buyback mechanisms. The goal is a mechanism-design treatment of how program rules shape who bids, how they bid, and the resulting efficiency of capacity retirement.
